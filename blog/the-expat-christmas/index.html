The Expat Christmas: When the Holidays Feel Far From Home

By Danielle Leite · 20 November 2026 · 5 min read

I can't count how many Christmases I've spent away from loved ones. It's been too many. Being away from family can really hurt from time to time, and the holidays are when it hurts the most.

Before I moved abroad, Christmas was predictable, in the best possible way. Every year we went to my grandparents', first one set and then the other, crossing Rio in the small hours. Christmas in my family was a huge party. My uncles would barbecue all night and Brazilian music played and played. It was the most fun I could imagine: Christmas, samba and the heat of high summer.

Fast forward to 1991, and I spent my first Christmas in the Northern Hemisphere, in the cold of York, England. Life didn't get more depressing than that. I remember a visceral feeling that first Christmas. I felt betrayed. What was the point of being there, when real life was happening somewhere else?

In the years since, I've spent some Christmases back home, even with my grandparents, who are long gone now. But I never again felt the utter contentment I had as a child. I'm sure that has less to do with which hemisphere I'm in and more to do with becoming the one who organises, buys the presents and cooks the big meal.

Over the decades, Christmas as an expat became something we celebrated in our own way. We created our own traditions and built our own families. That first Christmas in England may have been depressing, but soon we had our own little community, and the years after were something close to a real party. We made friends then whom we still see today. We travelled, we discovered different traditions, and we learned to enjoy Christmas away from home.

Now that I'm divorced, there's an extra layer of complication. I treasure the time I get with my children, and it's either Christmas or New Year, depending on the year before. The one thing that is, and always will be, missing is the big family celebration. That isn't even a reality for the family back home anymore: their numbers are shrinking, and travelling is hard for the older ones. I've learned to spend Christmas with a small but close group of loved ones, without the summer heat. Maybe that's more about getting older than about being an expat.

Life happens. We age, and we lose people we love. The question is whether we do it in the next town from where we were born, or while getting to see and know the world. When I add up the adventures I've had, the things I've seen and the friends I've made, they compare very well with the life I'd have had if I'd stayed in Rio. There's no version of my life I'd swap for this one. Being an expat has given me so much more than it has taken. I wouldn't have it any other way, even if I'm not in the tropical heat this Christmas.

When Christmas abroad feels heavy

The holidays have a way of magnifying everything. Social media fills up with family tables and homecomings, and everyone seems to be exactly where they belong. If you're far from home, it's easy to feel that you're the only one finding it hard. You're not.

You can love the life you've built abroad and still grieve the Christmases you've lost. Both are true at the same time, and neither cancels the other out. Missing home doesn't mean you made the wrong choice. It means something mattered to you.

For many expats, Christmas also brings up quieter losses: family members who are no longer there, a relationship that has ended, children who spend alternate years with the other parent, or the slow realisation that "home" has changed while you were away. It's no wonder so many people feel a little lost in December.

A few things can help:

  • Let it be different. Trying to recreate the Christmas of your childhood often makes the gap feel bigger. New traditions, however small, can become your own.
  • Choose your people. A small table of friends who know you can be as nourishing as a big family gathering.
  • Be kind to yourself. Feeling low at Christmas isn't ingratitude. Treat yourself the way you would treat a friend who was far from home.
  • Reach out before you need to. Many expats find it hard to ask for help, especially when everyone else seems busy and happy.
